- **Date:** 2026-10-15
- **Status:** Draft, awaiting user review
- **Extends:** `2026-10-14-rq0b-annual-break-dating-design.md` (Plan 1b, approved and merged into `plan-1-rq0b`)
- **Builds on:** Plan 1b code (`modeling/annual_breaks.py`, `us_state_annual`), decisions D-20 to D-28

## 1. Summary in plain terms

Plan 1b found a clear break in the state data: people stopped moving toward richer states. It couldn't say when.
- **The tie:** the best fit was 1990, but resampling split between 1990 (37%) and 1970 (30%).
- **What points to 1970:** without the oil states, or with income standardized within year, the best fit is 1970.

This plan tests two explanations for the tie:

1. **The oil boom hides a 1970 break.** In 1973–1990, oil states got rich and then poor, and people followed the oil jobs. That can look like "people follow money again" in the late 1970s, which pushes the best fit to 1990. **Test:** keep every state, add separate year effects for the energy states during the oil years, and compare "break in 1970" with "break in 1990" by fit.
2. **The baby boom fakes a break.** A state's population grows from births as well as moving. The baby boom ended in the mid-1960s. If richer and poorer states had different birth rates, total growth could shift around 1970 even if moving didn't. **Test:** measure **net migration** (population change minus births plus deaths) and run the same tests on it. Also test whether natural increase (births minus deaths) itself lines up with income differently before and after 1970.

**What success looks like:** a sentence for the paper of one of these forms:
- "Allowing for the oil cycle, a 1970 break fits clearly better than 1990, and it holds for net migration."
- "1990 holds up."
- Or a plain statement that the data still can't tell, and why.

## 2. Goals and non-goals

**Goals**
- An oil-cycle model on the annual state panel (1929–2024), with a fit comparison of 1970 vs. 1990 and the break search, test and range re-run with the oil terms.
- A state net-migration panel by decade (1940s–2010s) and by year (1968 on), and the RQ0b tests on it.
- A natural-increase check: does the link between births minus deaths and income shift around 1970?
- An updated RQ0b finding.

**Non-goals**
- **Census mover microdata (IPUMS):** saved for later, as the paper's confirmation step.
- **No typing up of the 1939–1967 annual birth and death tables** (option B in the 2026-10-15 discussion). It is revisited only if this plan shows births matter.
- **No metro version.** Metro data starts in 1969, too late to see 1970.
- **No new causal claims.** RQ0b stays descriptive.

- **Net migration definitions:**
  - Decade: `NM(i,d) = pop(d+10) − pop(d) − births(d..d+9) + deaths(d..d+9)`.
  - Year: `NM(i,t) = pop(t+1) − pop(t) − births(t) + deaths(t)`.
  - The rate divides by the starting population.
- **Two sources for the decade series:**
  - 1940s–1970s: the Census-published figures.
  - 1980s–2010s: computed from census counts and the annual births and deaths.
  - The 1970s can be computed both ways, and the two must agree (§6).
- **Timing mismatch:** BEA population is mid-year; births and deaths are calendar-year. The half-year offset is small next to the effects we are testing, and is stated as a limitation.
- **Same states as Plan 1b.** Alaska and Hawaii enter in 1950. The data rules of D-24 apply.

## 4. Methods

### 4.1 The oil model

On the annual state growth panel (as in Plan 1b §4.2), add a separate year effect for the energy states in each oil year:

`growth(i,t) = α_t + γ_t · E_i · [1973 ≤ t ≤ 1990] + β_before · x(i,t) · [t < T] + β_after · x(i,t) · [t ≥ T] + e(i,t)`

- **E_i** marks an energy state. The γ_t absorb whatever moved energy-state growth in each oil year, so the oil cycle can't show up as a change in β.
- **Fast estimation still works.** Each cell (a year, or a year × energy group in oil years) lies inside one segment, so demeaning within cells removes these effects exactly. The closed-form search from Plan 1b carries over; the demeaning step takes a cell column instead of the year.
- **Fit comparison:**
  - BIC for four models: {break at 1970, break at 1990} × {with, without the oil terms}.
  - The BIC gap between 1970 and 1990 with the oil terms is the headline number. A gap of 10 or more is conventionally "strong".
- **Search and test with the oil terms:** re-run the sup-Wald test, the break-year estimate and the bootstrap (999 draws) with the oil terms in. Report the vote share for each year; compare with Plan 1b's 37% / 30%.
- **Robustness:**
  - the hand-picked energy list;
  - a shorter oil window (1973–1986, boom to price collapse);
  - income standardized within year.

### 4.2 Net migration

- **Decade test:** the Plan 1 decade model (`breaks.decade_betas`, `chow_test`, `break_search`) with the net-migration rate in place of total growth, 1940–2010.
  - Figure: decade β for total growth and for net migration, side by side.
  - Reading: if the drop in β is still there around 1970 with births removed, the change is in moving.
- **Annual test, 1968 on:** the Plan 1b machinery on the annual net-migration rate. Like the metro data, it starts too late to place a 1970 break. It checks whether the post-1970 picture differs when births are removed (for example, whether a 1990 break survives).
- **Natural-increase check:** the decade model with the natural-increase rate ((births − deaths) ÷ population) as the outcome.
  - If its link to income shifts around 1970, the baby boom was distorting the total-growth test.
  - If it doesn't, the Plan 1b result is about moving.

### 4.3 What gets reported

One table, `reports/tables/rq0b_1970_vs_1990.csv`. Its columns are `test, outcome, spec, statistic, value, detail`, covering:
- the four BICs;
- the searched break year with the oil terms, its range and vote shares;
- sup-Wald against its critical value;
- the decade net-migration break tests;
- the natural-increase check.

One figure, `reports/figures/rq0b_net_migration_betas.png`. A new notebook 1.1 section, "1970 or 1990?", ends with a plain finding.

## 6. Testing

- **Synthetic recovery (fast):**
  - A panel with a planted 1970 break plus a planted oil episode in 8 of 50 units (1973–1990):
    - without the oil terms, the search is pulled away from 1970;
    - with them, it finds 1970 ± 2, and the BIC prefers 1970 to 1990.
  - A planted 1990 break with no oil episode: the oil model still picks 1990.
- **Exactness:** demeaning by cell gives the same slopes, SSR and clustered Wald as a regression with explicit year and year × energy dummies (to 1e-8). This is the same kind of check as Plan 1b's.
- **Net-migration identity:** on a toy panel, NM + births − deaths reproduces the population change exactly. A missing births or deaths value drops the unit-period; it is never filled.
- **Data tests:**
  - Hand-entered Census figures: each decade's sum over states is within 1% of the published US net migration for that decade (state flows cancel out, leaving net international migration), and each state's entry passes an identity check against census population change and published births and deaths where those are printed.
  - The 1970s two ways: computed vs. published net migration correlate above 0.95 across states, and differ by less than 1% of 1970 population on average.
  - Annual births and deaths: the national totals for 1970, 1980, …, 2020 are within 0.5% of the published US totals.

## 7. Risks

| Risk | Mitigation |
|---|---|
| The oil terms absorb too much: with 8 energy states, γ_t uses up their within-year variation | Hand list and short-window checks; the oil terms only cover 1973–1990 |
| A BIC gap that is small or flips with the robustness checks | Reported as "still can't tell", with the gap sizes |
| Hand-entry errors in the Census tables | Sum and identity checks (§6); the 1970s computed both ways |
| No annual births and deaths by state from a usable source for 1968+ | The first plan task confirms the source. Fallback: the decade analysis only, with 1980s+ from published Census components |
| Mid-year vs. calendar-year timing | Small next to the effects tested; stated as a limitation |
| Net migration includes international migration, which isn't "moving toward money" in the same sense | Reported as a caveat. International arrivals concentrate in a few states, so the net-migration tests are also run without CA, NY, FL and TX as a robustness row |
